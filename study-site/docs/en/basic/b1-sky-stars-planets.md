## What a star is

A **star** is a huge ball of gas, almost entirely hydrogen and helium, held together by its own gravity. Gravity pulls everything toward the center. At the center, pressure and temperature get so high (about 15 million degrees in the Sun) that hydrogen nuclei fuse and become helium. This **nuclear fusion** releases energy. The energy leaves through the surface as light and heat.

It is a balance: gravity squeezes in, and the energy from fusion pushes out. As long as there is hydrogen to burn at the center, the star stays stable for billions of years. The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old and roughly halfway through this phase. Every second it turns about 600 million tons of hydrogen into helium.

If you are looking for planets, stability matters: a calm star has an almost constant brightness, and it is against this constant background that we look for tiny dips.

## Types of star: color, temperature and size

Look at the sky on a dark night and you will see stars of different colors: bluish, white, yellowish, orange. The color tells you the **surface temperature**, the same way heated metal goes from red to white as it gets hotter.

Astronomers sort stars into **spectral types**, labeled with letters. The order, from hottest to coolest, is O, B, A, F, G, K, M.

| Type | Apparent color | Surface temperature | Typical size (stable phase) | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| O | blue | above 30,000 K | much larger than the Sun | rare |
| B | bluish-white | 10,000 to 30,000 K | larger than the Sun | — |
| A | white | 7,500 to 10,000 K | 1.5 to 2.5 R☉ | Sirius A |
| F | yellowish-white | 6,000 to 7,500 K | 1.2 to 1.6 R☉ | — |
| G | yellow | 5,200 to 6,000 K | about 1 R☉ | the Sun (5,772 K) |
| K | orange | 3,700 to 5,200 K | 0.7 to 0.9 R☉ | Alpha Centauri B |
| M | red | 2,400 to 3,700 K | 0.1 to 0.6 R☉ | Proxima Centauri (0.15 R☉) |

K stands for **kelvin**, the temperature scale physicists use. For stars, kelvin and degrees Celsius hardly differ (there are only 273 degrees between them).

Three families show up all the time in this course:

- **Red dwarfs** (type M). Small, cool, dim. They are the most common stars in the galaxy: about three out of every four. They live much longer than the Sun.
- **Sun-like stars** (types F, G and K). The classic target in the search for "other Earths".
- **Giants**. Stars that have used up the hydrogen in their centers and swelled up, reaching tens or hundreds of times the Sun's radius. Giants are hard for planet searches: as you will see in lesson B2, a big star hides small planets.

!!! info "Fun fact"
    The "V" in classifications like "G2 V" means that the star is in its stable phase, burning hydrogen at its center. Astronomers call this phase the **main sequence**. The Sun is a G2 V star.

## The units of the field

Distances and sizes in space are so large that kilometers get awkward. That is why the field uses familiar objects as rulers.

| Unit | Symbol | Value | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solar radius | R☉ | 696,000 km | size of stars |
| Solar mass | M☉ | 2 × 10³⁰ kg (333,000 Earths) | mass of stars |
| Earth radius | R⊕ | 6,371 km | size of planets |
| Jupiter radius | R_J | 69,911 km (about 11 R⊕) | giant planets |
| Astronomical unit | AU | 150 million km | Earth-Sun distance; size of orbits |
| Light-year | — | 9.46 trillion km | distance to stars |
| Parsec | pc | 3.26 light-years | distance to stars (professional use) |

Some relations worth memorizing:

- The Sun's radius is about **109 R⊕**. You can line up 109 Earths across the Sun's radius.
- Jupiter's radius is about **11 R⊕**, and the Sun's radius is about 10 Jupiter radii.
- The nearest star after the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about **4.2 light-years** away. Its light takes 4.2 years to reach us.

A **light-year** is the distance light travels in one year. Despite the name, it is a distance, not a time. The **parsec** is the unit astronomers prefer in papers, because it comes from a geometric measurement (parallax). For this course, you only need to know that 1 parsec equals 3.26 light-years.

Here are the relations written in symbols, which is how you will find them in formulas:

$$ 1\,R_\odot \approx 109\,R_\oplus \qquad 1\,R_J \approx 11\,R_\oplus \qquad 1\,\text{pc} \approx 3.26\ \text{light-years} $$

!!! example "Exercise 1"
    Jupiter's radius is 69,911 km. Express this radius in R⊕ and in R☉.

    ??? success "Show answer"
        In Earth radii: $69{,}911 / 6{,}371 \approx 11.0$, so Jupiter is about **11 R⊕**.

        In solar radii: $69{,}911 / 696{,}000 \approx 0.100$, so Jupiter is about **0.1 R☉**, one tenth of the Sun's radius.

        Remember this 0.1. In the next lesson you will see that it explains why the Sun's light would drop by about 1% if Jupiter passed in front of it: $0.1^2 = 0.01$.

## What an exoplanet is

An **exoplanet** is a planet that orbits a star other than the Sun. The idea is old, but the confirmation is recent:

- **1992**: the first planets outside the Solar System, around a pulsar (the corpse of a star that exploded). A very strange environment.
- **1995**: Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz announce 51 Pegasi b, the first planet around a Sun-like star. It was a "hot Jupiter": a gas giant in an orbit of just over 4 days. The work earned the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics.
- **2009 to 2018**: NASA's **Kepler** space telescope watches a single region of the sky for four years and shows that planets are common: most stars have at least one.
- **2018 onward**: **TESS**, Kepler's successor, starts observing almost the entire sky. This is where PlanetHunter gets its data (lesson B3).

In 2026 about **6,400 confirmed exoplanets** are known, according to the NASA Exoplanet Archive. Thousands of **candidates** are waiting for confirmation.

Exoplanets come in sizes the Solar System does not have. The most common ones in the galaxy, between 1 and 4 R⊕, do not exist here: **super-Earths** (rocky, larger than Earth) and **mini-Neptunes** (with a thick atmosphere, smaller than Neptune). There are also **hot Jupiters**, giants hugging their star, with orbits of just a few days.

!!! question "Question to think about"
    If most stars have planets, why did it take until 1995 to find the first one around a Sun-like star? Think about it as you read the next section.

## How do you find a planet nobody can see

Almost no exoplanet can be photographed. It is too far away, and its star is billions of times brighter than it. So astronomers look for **effects** that the planet has on the star or on its light. There are four main methods.

### 1. Transit: the fly in front of the flashlight

Picture a flashlight at the end of a dark hallway and a fly passing in front of it. You cannot see the fly, but a sensitive light meter notices that the flashlight got a tiny bit dimmer for a few moments.

When a planet passes in front of its star, as seen from where we are, the brightness drops a little. This is a **transit**. If the dip repeats at regular intervals, we get the orbital period. From the amount of light blocked, we estimate the planet's **size**. This is the topic of lesson B2 and the method PlanetHunter uses.

### 2. Radial velocity: the owner pulled by the dog

A big dog running in circles around its owner, held by a leash, makes the owner sway back and forth. You can see the owner swaying even without seeing the dog.

A planet and its star both circle a common center. The star wobbles, moving toward us and away from us. When it moves toward us, its light becomes slightly bluer; when it moves away, slightly redder. It is the same effect that changes the pitch of an ambulance siren as it drives past (the **Doppler effect**). By measuring this back-and-forth in the star's light, we estimate the planet's **mass**. Jupiter makes the Sun wobble at about 12.5 m/s; Earth, at only 9 cm/s. This is how 51 Pegasi b was found.

### 3. Direct imaging: the firefly next to the lighthouse

Imagine photographing a firefly a few centimeters from a lit lighthouse, seen from kilometers away. It can be done, with instruments that block the star's light, but only for planets that are large, young (still hot, glowing in the infrared) and far from their star. There are only a few such cases.

### 4. Gravitational microlensing: the passing magnifying glass

A star's gravity bends light that passes close to it, like a lens. When a star passes exactly in front of another, more distant one, it acts like a magnifying glass and the background star gets brighter for a few days. If the foreground star has a planet, the planet causes a brief "hiccup" in that rise. The alignment is extremely rare and **does not repeat**: each planet is seen only once.

### Comparing the four

| Method | What it measures | Analogy | Advantage | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Transit | dip in brightness | fly in front of the flashlight | measures the radius; many stars at once | only works if the orbit is aligned with us |
| Radial velocity | wobble of the star | owner pulled by the dog | measures the mass | one star at a time; needs a large telescope and a spectrograph |
| Direct imaging | light from the planet itself | firefly next to the lighthouse | actually sees the planet | only young giants far from their star |
| Microlensing | increase in brightness | passing magnifying glass | finds planets far from their star and far from us | one-off event, never repeats |

## Why the transit method dominates

About three out of every four confirmed exoplanets were found by transits. Radial velocity comes second, with close to one fifth. Direct imaging and microlensing together account for a few percent. Three reasons explain the advantage of transits:

1. **Scale.** One camera measures the brightness of tens of thousands of stars at the same time. Radial velocity requires observing one star at a time.
2. **Dedicated telescopes.** Kepler and TESS were built only for this, and they produced continuous, high-precision data for hundreds of thousands of stars.
3. **Planet size.** A transit gives you the radius. Combine it with the mass (from radial velocity) and you get the **density**: you can tell whether the planet is rocky or gaseous.

But there is a price, and it is the central theme of this course: **many things imitate a transit**. Two stars eclipsing each other, a neighboring star changing in brightness, a flaw in the telescope. That is why a transit signal on its own is never a planet. It has to survive tests (lesson B4) and, in the end, be confirmed by another method, usually radial velocity.

!!! example "Exercise 2"
    For each situation, say which method fits best and why.

    1. You want to measure the size of thousands of short-period planets all at once.
    2. You have a transit candidate and want to know whether it is rocky or gaseous.
    3. You want to find planets in very wide orbits, around stars thousands of light-years away, in the center of the galaxy.

    ??? success "Show answer"
        1. **Transit.** It measures the radius and lets you observe many stars at the same time. A short period helps: more transits during the observation time.
        2. **Radial velocity.** The transit already gave you the radius. The mass is missing. With radius and mass you can calculate the density: high density (like Earth's, 5.5 g/cm³) points to rock; low density points to a lot of gas.
        3. **Microlensing.** It does not depend on the brightness of the star that hosts the planet (which may be faint and far away), and it is sensitive to wide orbits. The downside: the event happens only once.

!!! example "Exercise 3"
    How many Earths, lined up side by side, would span the Sun's **diameter**? And how many Jupiters?

    ??? success "Show answer"
        The diameter is twice the radius for both objects, so the ratio is the same as for the radii.

        - Earths: $696{,}000 / 6{,}371 \approx 109$. **109 Earths** fit across the Sun's diameter.
        - Jupiters: $696{,}000 / 69{,}911 \approx 10$. **10 Jupiters** fit.

        This ratio of radii, $R_p / R_\star$, is the key piece of the next lesson.
